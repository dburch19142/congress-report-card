Look at the list of bills that have become law in the 119th Congress and one kind of title appears again and again: "Providing for congressional disapproval under chapter 8 of title 5, United States Code, of the rule submitted by..." Twenty-three laws sponsored by current members have that form. Each one cancelled a regulation that a federal agency had already issued. Together they make up almost a fifth of the 126 laws on the full list.

These laws come from the Congressional Review Act, a 1996 law that gives Congress a fast way to overturn agency rules. Understanding how it works explains why so many of these resolutions passed, and why they cluster at the start of a new administration.

What the Congressional Review Act does

Federal agencies write regulations that carry out the laws Congress passes. Before a major rule takes effect, the agency must send it to Congress. The Congressional Review Act then gives Congress a window to reject the rule by passing a joint resolution of disapproval. Like any law, the resolution needs to pass both chambers and be signed by the President, or survive a veto.

What makes the act powerful is its procedure in the Senate. Most legislation in the Senate effectively needs 60 votes, because that is what it takes to end a filibuster. A disapproval resolution under the Congressional Review Act cannot be filibustered, and debate on it is limited, so it can pass with a simple majority. In a closely divided Senate, that difference is decisive.

A disapproved rule doesn't just disappear. The act also bars the agency from issuing a new rule that is "substantially the same" unless Congress later authorizes it. That makes a disapproval more lasting than a rule change made by a later administration, which can itself be reversed.

Why these resolutions cluster at the start of a term

For most of the act's history, it was rarely used successfully. The reason is simple: a President won't usually sign a resolution cancelling a rule written by their own administration. Disapprovals succeed when a new President of a different party takes office alongside a friendly Congress and the previous administration's late rules are still within reach.

The act includes a "lookback" provision that makes this possible. Rules submitted to Congress in roughly the last 60 days that the House or Senate was in session before the end of a session can be reviewed again in the next session, as though they had just been submitted. Rules finalized in the last months of an outgoing administration therefore remain open to disapproval in the first months of the next one. That is what happened in 2017, and it is what happened again in 2025.

What Congress disapproved this time

The 23 regulatory disapprovals sponsored by current members fall into a few groups.

  • Land management plans. Several resolutions overturned Bureau of Land Management resource management plans and decisions, including plans for the Central Yukon region and the National Petroleum Reserve in Alaska, the Miles City field office in Montana, the North Dakota field office and the Buffalo field office in Wyoming, along with the Coastal Plain oil and gas leasing program in Alaska and a public land withdrawal order in northern Minnesota.
  • Energy efficiency standards. Several Department of Energy rules setting efficiency standards for appliances and equipment were cancelled.
  • Environmental rules. Resolutions overturned Environmental Protection Agency actions including three waivers that allowed California to set its own vehicle and engine emissions standards, the waste emissions charge for oil and gas facilities, and a hazardous air pollutant standard.
  • Financial rules. Congress cancelled an IRS rule requiring decentralized cryptocurrency platforms to report customer transactions, two Consumer Financial Protection Bureau rules, one on overdraft fees at very large banks and one bringing large digital payment apps under federal supervision, and a rule from the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency.
  • Other rules. The list also includes a Bureau of Ocean Energy Management rule on marine archaeological resources and a National Park Service rule on motor vehicle use in the Glen Canyon National Recreation Area.

Seventeen of the 23 were sponsored in the House and six in the Senate. The land management resolutions were all sponsored by members from the states affected: Alaska, Montana, North Dakota, Wyoming and Minnesota. Two members sponsored more than one successful disapproval: [[Nicholas J. Begich III]] of Alaska and [[John Kennedy]] of Louisiana.

A related power: reviewing District of Columbia laws

One more disapproval resolution on the list is different. Under the District of Columbia Home Rule Act, laws passed by the D.C. Council go to Congress for review before they take effect, and Congress can block them by passing a joint resolution of disapproval. This Congress used that power to overturn a D.C. law conforming the District's income and franchise taxes to federal changes. The resolution was sponsored by [[Brandon Gill]] of Texas. Congress rarely uses this power successfully, though it has done so more often in recent years.

How these laws affect members' grades

A disapproval resolution that becomes law counts the same as any other bill on a member's report card: 10 points under "bills written." That has a visible effect. A single law is worth as much as ten bills that were introduced and went nowhere, so sponsors of successful disapproval resolutions get a real boost in their legislative score.

Is that fair? Congress Report Card's grades measure legislative results, not the merits of a policy, and a joint resolution that becomes law is a law. Sponsoring one takes the same work any bill does: drafting, building support, and getting floor time in both chambers. At the same time, these resolutions are easier to pass than ordinary bills because of their protected Senate procedure, and almost all of them are sponsored by members of the party that controls Congress and the White House. Readers can weigh that context themselves. The member pages list every bill that advanced, so you can see whether a member's laws were disapprovals, spending bills or standalone policy.

What comes next

The lookback window for rules from the previous administration has closed, so the pace of disapprovals has slowed. Congress can still disapprove rules issued by the current administration, but that rarely succeeds when the President's own party controls Congress. The next large wave, if there is one, would come at the start of the next change in administration.
